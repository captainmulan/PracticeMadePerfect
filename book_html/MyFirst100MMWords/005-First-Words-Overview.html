Welcome to Your First Myanmar Words

Ancient stories, gentle morals, and tap-to-hear words — written for English-reading children.

Chapter one · The beginning

The Girl Who Listened at Dawn

In a teak village where mist rose from the Irrawaddy each morning, a girl named May sat on her grandmother's porch with a parabaik scroll spread between them. The scroll showed pictures — rice, elephants, pagodas, and smiling faces — but no English letters, because long ago Myanmar children learned with eyes and ears before they learned with books. Grandmother pointed to each picture and spoke a word. May repeated the sound, not worrying yet about the curved script she could not read. "You are learning the way river children always learned," Grandmother said. "Hear the word, hold the meaning, and let the writing come later like footprints after rain." When the sun touched the golden spire of the distant pagoda, May could name ten things in the language of her ancestors. She did not feel like a student in a hurry. She felt like a child keeping a lamp alive — one word at a time, one story at a time. That is how this book works for you too: read the English story, look at the picture, then tap the word cards to hear Myanmar spoken aloud. You never need to read Myanmar script in the stories. Your ears will do the first work, and your heart will remember.
Chapter two · The storyteller

The Teacher Under the Banyan Tree

Before schools of brick and chalk, Myanmar villages kept their wisdom under banyan trees. An old storyteller named U Ba would ring a small bell, and children would gather with palm-leaf fans and quiet feet. He never began with grammar. He began with a moral tale — about a generous merchant, a proud tiger, or a monk who shared his last bowl of rice. "Language lives inside stories," U Ba told them. "A word you steal from a list may vanish by evening. A word you meet in a story may stay for life." He taught family words when he told of Thadingyut candles carried to grandparents. He taught animal words when he sang of the Four Friends who saved one another in a flood. He taught festival words when he described Thingyan water blessing the year clean again. Parents who use this book carry on U Ba's method. Read the English tale aloud at bedtime. Let your child tap the Myanmar button on each word card once — not fifty times in a panic, but once with care, like placing a candle on a shrine. The goal is not to memorize a dictionary. The goal is to belong to a language that has been spoken with kindness for centuries.
Chapter three · The four friends

Why Animals Appear in Every Chapter

Myanmar folktales are full of animals who talk, argue, and forgive. The most beloved is the tale of the Four Friends: Elephant, Monkey, Rabbit, and Peacock. They are different in size and strength, yet when the forest flooded they did not ask who was most important. Elephant carried Rabbit. Monkey cleared the path. Peacock searched for dry land. Together they survived. The moral traveled from village to village for hundreds of years: friends who help one another need not be alike. That is also true of languages. You may speak English at school and hear Myanmar at home. You may live far from Yangon yet still learn the words your grandparents whispered over tea. The two languages are not enemies. They can be friends, like the animals in the forest. In this book you will meet ten worlds — family, food, animals, colors, numbers, body, home, school, feelings, and festivals. Each world has a big picture, a moral story in English, word cards to tap, a mini game, and a quiz. Turn the page from this overview and choose any chapter from the index. There is no single correct order. Follow the story that makes your eyes bright. The words will follow.

How to use this book

  • Each chapter has three picture · story · explanation blocks, then word cards and a game.
  • Read every story in English — no Myanmar script is required anywhere.
  • Tap 🔊 Hear on a word card to hear spoken Myanmar (English label stays on screen).
  • Play the catch game at the bottom of each chapter to earn badges.
  • Visit the Explained page for a second picture, a longer tale, and ten speaking sentences.
  • Parents: read the tip on each page — it links old Myanmar customs to today's practice.
